Short Statement

In a two-sided platform where a fixed flexibility budget can be split between the two sides of a random bipartite graph, is the expected maximum matching always optimized by either the fully one-sided or the balanced allocation?

Open Problem

Prove or refute that, in the Freund-Martin-Zhao random bipartite flexibility model, the expected maximum matching is always maximized by either a fully one-sided flexibility allocation or a balanced allocation, and characterize the boundary between the regimes.

Background & Context

Process flexibility is a central operations-management idea: a little flexibility in who can serve whom can recover much of the value of full flexibility. Traditional models usually allocate flexibility on one side of a bipartite system, such as plants to products or servers to demand classes. Two-sided platforms complicate this because compatibility can be improved on either side: drivers may become able to serve more rider types, or riders may become willing to accept more provider types. Freund, Martin, and Zhao propose a parsimonious random bipartite-graph model where a fixed flexibility budget can be split across sides and performance is the expected maximum matching size. Their analysis shows that optimizing each side separately can be badly misleading, and that two forces, flexibility cannibalization and flexibility abundance, help explain when one-sided or balanced allocations are good. The unresolved part is a sharp structural theorem for the true matching objective.

Statement & Model

Consider the heterogeneous random bipartite-graph model of Freund, Martin, and Zhao. There are two sides of a matching market, a fixed budget BBB of flexible agents, and an allocation decision specifying how much flexibility is placed on each side. Flexible nodes have a higher probability of being compatible with nodes on the opposite side, while the model normalizes edge probabilities so that changes in performance come from how compatibility is distributed rather than simply from adding more expected edges. The main performance metric μ\muμ is the asymptotic expected size of a maximum matching. Numerical evidence using the Karp-Sipser-based approximation μKS\mu_{KS}μKS​ and empirical simulations μEMP\mu_{EMP}μEMP​ suggests that μ\muμ is always maximized either by the one-sided allocation or by the balanced allocation. The open problem is to prove this extremal principle, or find a counterexample, and then give an analytical boundary in (B,pn,pf)(B,p_n,p_f)(B,pn​,pf​) space separating the one-sided-optimal and balanced-optimal regimes.

What Is Known

Freund, Martin, and Zhao fully analyze a simpler isolation-based performance metric and derive partial comparisons for the maximum-matching objective in edge regimes. For the actual expected maximum matching size, their Karp-Sipser approximation and simulations suggest a striking dichotomy: optimal allocations appear to be either one-sided or balanced, not intermediate. The paper identifies flexibility cannibalization and flexibility abundance as drivers of the two regimes, but a proof of the extremal dichotomy and an analytical phase boundary remain open.

Progress & Literature

Latest progress (Partial progress). Isolation-based metrics are analytically characterized and maximum-matching edge regimes are partially understood. The full expected-matching extremal principle is still conjectural. Freund, Martin, Zhao, Two-Sided Flexibility in Platforms

The classical process-flexibility literature shows that sparse flexibility designs can be nearly as valuable as full flexibility, but it usually treats flexibility as located on one side. The two-sided model introduces a new allocation dimension: flexibility on the two sides can substitute for or cannibalize each other. Freund-Martin-Zhao's analytical isolation metric gives intuition, while the maximum-matching metric is the operationally central objective. The remaining gap is mathematical: prove that the observed one-sided/balanced dichotomy is inherent to the random matching model, or find a parameter regime where an interior split is strictly optimal.
