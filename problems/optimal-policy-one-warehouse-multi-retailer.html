Optimal policy for one-warehouse multi-retailer (distribution) inventory systems

Short Statement

A single warehouse ships, with positive lead times, to several retailers each facing independent random demand. The optimal replenishment-and-allocation policy is unknown: echelon base-stock is optimal only under the artificial balance assumption (which permits negative allocations); without it, the true optimal structure is open.

Open Problem

Characterize the optimal replenishment-and-allocation policy for one-warehouse multi-retailer inventory under the true nonnegative-allocation constraints.

Background & Context

Multi-echelon inventory theory began with Clark and Scarf's 1960 proof that for SERIAL supply chains — a single chain of stages, each feeding the next — the optimal policy has a beautifully simple structure: each stage follows an echelon base-stock policy, ordering up to a fixed target measured in echelon inventory position. The natural next topology, and by far the most common in practice, is the divergent (distribution) system: one warehouse replenishes from an outside supplier and ships to NNN retailers, each facing its own random demand. Here the Clark–Scarf decomposition breaks. The culprit is the allocation problem: once stock is shipped to a retailer it cannot be moved sideways to a sister retailer, so when demands realize unevenly the system becomes imbalanced — one retailer is overstocked while another is starved — and the warehouse cannot simultaneously bring all echelon inventory positions to their desired levels. Eppen and Schrage (1981) and then Federgruen and Zipkin (1984) showed that if one relaxes the model to permit negative shipments (the so-called balance or allocation assumption, effectively free lateral transshipment), the problem decomposes and echelon base-stock policies become optimal; Diks and de Kok (1998) extended this to general nnn-echelon divergent networks. But the relaxation is a fiction, and Zipkin (1984) analyzed exactly how inventory imbalance degrades these approximations. Without the balance assumption, echelon base-stock policies are provably suboptimal, the dynamic program does not decompose, and after six decades no well-structured optimal policy is known — a gap flagged as a central open issue in every major survey of the field. The problem matters enormously in practice: essentially every retail and spare-parts distribution network is divergent, and the industry runs on heuristics (allocation rules like fair-share rationing, projected-inventory policies, Lagrangian decompositions) whose optimality gaps are typically assessed only numerically. A structural characterization, or a simple policy with a provable small constant-factor guarantee for the stochastic backlog objective, would close one of the oldest open questions in inventory theory.

What Is Known

Structure under relaxation: echelon base-stock (order-up-to) policies are optimal for divergent systems ONLY under the balance assumption, which permits negative allocations to retailers (Eppen–Schrage 1981; Federgruen–Zipkin 1984; Diks–de Kok, EJOR 1998, for general nnn-echelon divergent networks). Under the true constraint qi≥0q_i \ge 0qi​≥0 the problem does not decompose, echelon base-stock is suboptimal, and no well-structured optimal policy is known — de Kok, Grob, Laumanns, Minner, Rambau, Schade (EJOR 2018) state flatly that 'so far, it has been impossible to identify well-structured optimal policies for n-echelon divergent systems without this assumption,' and a mid-2026 check finds no paper overturning this. Asymptotic/approximation progress for the STOCHASTIC problem: Miao, Jasin, Chao (Operations Research 70(1):141–159, 2022) give Lagrangian-relaxation-based policies for one-warehouse (and multi-warehouse) multi-store systems with lost sales whose loss relative to optimal is sublinear in both the horizon TTT and the number of stores NNN — asymptotic optimality as the system grows, but no fixed-instance constant-factor guarantee, and for the lost-sales rather than backlog objective. Najy, Diabat, Elbassioni (Operations Research 73(3):1151–1164, 2025) give an FPTAS for the continuous relaxation of the stochastic OWMR under the (S,T)(S,T)(S,T)/power-of-two policy class (building on Chu–Shen's 2010 approximate cost model) with an improved rounding factor to power-of-two solutions — approximation within a restricted policy class of an approximate model, not the true optimum. For the DETERMINISTIC lot-sizing OWMR, the best general constant remains the 1.8-approximation of Levi, Roundy, Shmoys, Sviridenko (Management Science 54(4):763–776, 2008; incorporating the Levi–Sviridenko APPROX 2006 improvement); the JRP special case has a 1.791-approximation (Bienkowski, Byrka, Chrobak, Jeż, Nogneng, Sgall, SODA 2014), and Segev (Mathematics of Operations Research, 2025; arXiv:2408.05443) recently gave the first improvement over power-of-two-type guarantees for continuous-time joint replenishment. As of July 2026, neither an optimal-policy characterization nor a constant-factor approximation for the stochastic divergent system with the no-negative-shipment constraint is known.

Progress & Literature

Latest progress (Partial progress). No structural breakthrough as of July 2026: the optimal policy for divergent systems without the balance assumption remains uncharacterized. Recent progress is asymptotic and computational rather than structural: Miao–Jasin–Chao (Operations Research 2022) proved Lagrangian-relaxation policies asymptotically optimal (loss sublinear in horizon and number of stores) for one-/multi-warehouse multi-store systems with lost sales, and Najy–Diabat–Elbassioni (Operations Research 2025) gave an FPTAS-based method computing near-optimal power-of-two (S,T)(S,T)(S,T) policies for an approximate stochastic OWMR model. On the deterministic side the 1.8-approximation of Levi–Roundy–Shmoys–Sviridenko (2008) is still the best general constant for OWMR; Segev (Math. OR 2025) improved continuous-time joint replenishment guarantees beyond power-of-two policies, suggesting the OWMR constant may also be improvable. Work on divergent systems splits into three strands. (1) Structure under relaxation: Eppen–Schrage (1981) and Federgruen–Zipkin (1984) showed the balance assumption (allowing negative allocations) restores the Clark–Scarf decomposition, making echelon base-stock policies optimal; Diks–de Kok (1998) generalized this to nnn-echelon divergent networks, and Zipkin (1984) quantified how real imbalance erodes these approximations. This line has been essentially frozen since the 1990s: the de Kok et al. (2018) survey records that no well-structured optimal policy is known without the assumption. (2) Asymptotics for the true stochastic system: Miao–Jasin–Chao (OR 2022) is the state of the art, proving Lagrangian-relaxation policies for one-/multi-warehouse multi-store lost-sales systems have optimality loss sublinear in both the horizon and the number of stores — the first rigorous performance guarantee for the unrelaxed allocation dynamics, but asymptotic rather than uniform, and for lost sales rather than backlogging. (3) Approximation algorithms for the deterministic lot-sizing OWMR: Levi–Roundy–Shmoys–Sviridenko (MS 2008) hold the best general constant at 1.8 via LP rounding; JRP stands at 1.791 (Bienkowski et al., SODA 2014); and Segev (Math. OR 2025) broke the long-standing power-of-two barrier for continuous-time JRP, with Najy–Diabat–Elbassioni (OR 2025) contributing an FPTAS for computing power-of-two (S,T)(S,T)(S,T) policies in an approximate stochastic OWMR model. The remaining gap is stark: nothing connects strands (2)–(3) to a fixed-instance constant-factor guarantee — let alone a structural optimality result — for the stochastic backlog divergent system with the no-lateral-transshipment constraint. New ideas seem needed either in coupling/cost-accounting arguments that tame imbalance directly, or in identifying a richer but still tractable policy class (state-dependent allocation indices) provably close to the DP optimum.
