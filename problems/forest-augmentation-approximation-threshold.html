Short Statement

Forest Augmentation now has a better-than-2 approximation, but the correct approximation threshold for this basic survivable-network-design problem is still unknown.

Open Problem

Determine the best possible polynomial-time approximation ratio for unweighted Forest Augmentation, or prove a strong hardness/integrality-gap barrier matching the best algorithms.

Background & Context

Survivable network design asks for cheap networks that remain connected after failures. The general framework is broad, but augmentation problems isolate the essential combinatorics: start with a graph that is almost connected enough and add links to reach the desired connectivity. Tree Augmentation, where the initial graph is connected, has seen increasingly refined algorithms, including improvements for weighted variants. Forest Augmentation is only a small step away syntactically, but disconnected components remove structural tools that rely on a single tree. The 2021 breakthrough showed that factor 2 is not intrinsic even here, using a reduction to path augmentation and a credit argument. That leaves a clean OR-style approximation problem: identify the actual constant and the right relaxation or hard family.

Statement & Model

In the unweighted Forest Augmentation Problem, the input is an undirected forest F=(V,EF)F=(V,E_F)F=(V,EF​) and a set LLL of additional candidate links on the same vertex set. The goal is to choose a minimum-cardinality subset S⊆LS\subseteq LS⊆L such that the graph (V,EF∪S)(V,E_F\cup S)(V,EF​∪S) is 2-edge-connected. This is one of the cleanest survivable-network-design augmentation models beyond Tree Augmentation. Jain's iterative-rounding framework gives a factor-2 approximation for broad survivable network design problems, and for years this was the benchmark for Forest Augmentation. Grandoni, Jabal Ameli, and Traub breached the factor-2 barrier for FAP by reducing to and solving a path-augmentation core. The open problem is to determine the true polynomial-time approximation threshold for Forest Augmentation, and more broadly to understand how far below the Jain factor 222 one can go for basic augmentation problems whose starting graph is disconnected.

What Is Known

Jain's iterative rounding gives a 2-approximation for broad survivable network design settings, and this bound applied to Forest Augmentation. Better-than-2 algorithms were known for Tree Augmentation and related connected-start cases, but those methods did not transfer directly to forests. Grandoni, Jabal Ameli, and Traub give the first better-than-2 approximation for Forest Augmentation and also solve a better-than-2 Path Augmentation subproblem that had been open. The exact achieved constant is not presented as a tight threshold, and known hardness for the clean unweighted FAP model is far from a matching barrier. Thus the remaining open part is to pin down the approximability constant or identify a relaxation whose gap explains the limit. The earlier FAP ratio is 1.99731.99731.9973. Hommelsheim's 2025 preprint improves it to 1.99551.99551.9955 (Theorem 2), using a 1.94121.94121.9412 Path Augmentation guarantee (Theorem 1). The exact unweighted FAP threshold remains open.

Progress & Literature

Latest progress (Partial progress). Better-than-222 is already known; the reviewed 2025 preprint improves FAP from 1.99731.99731.9973 to 1.99551.99551.9955. The tight threshold remains open. Felix Hommelsheim, Improved Approximation Algorithms for Path and Forest Augmentation via a Novel Relaxation, arXiv:2505.15324 (2025)

Forest Augmentation is valuable because it is neither an arbitrary survivable-network-design problem nor merely Tree Augmentation in disguise. Jain's 2-approximation sets the general benchmark. The connected-tree case has a richer structure based on shadows, stems, compound witnesses, and rewiring; the disconnected forest case requires controlling how links simultaneously repair different components. Grandoni, Jabal Ameli, and Traub's path-augmentation reduction is the first evidence that the disconnected-start case still has exploitable structure below factor 2. What remains unclear is whether this is a modest improvement or the beginning of a much lower threshold. A tight answer would likely clarify the limits of iterative rounding and credit-based methods across survivable network design.
