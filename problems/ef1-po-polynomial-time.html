Short Statement

For indivisible goods with additive valuations, an allocation that is both EF1 (envy-free up to one good) and Pareto-optimal always exists, but the known general algorithms are only pseudo-polynomial. Can such an allocation be computed in time polynomial in the number of agents, the number of goods, and the bit-length of the values?

Open Problem

Determine whether a truly polynomial-time algorithm exists; as of mid-2026 this is open for general nnn, resolved affirmatively only when nnn is constant or the agents have constantly many distinct values.

Background & Context

A central theme in discrete fair division is whether fairness and economic efficiency can be achieved simultaneously, and at what computational cost. For indivisible goods with additive valuations, envy-freeness up to one good (EF1) — introduced by Budish (2011) and, implicitly, Lipton et al. (2004) — is the standard fairness relaxation: any envy an agent feels toward another must vanish after hypothetically removing some single good from the envied bundle. Pareto optimality (PO), and its strictly stronger fractional variant fPO (no fractional reallocation Pareto-dominates the allocation), are the standard efficiency notions. Each property is trivial to achieve alone (round-robin gives EF1; welfare maximization gives PO), but combining them is subtle. The landmark result of Caragiannis, Kurokawa, Moulin, Procaccia, Shah, and Wang (EC 2016) showed that any allocation maximizing Nash social welfare is simultaneously EF1 and PO — a striking existence proof, but computationally useless since maximizing Nash welfare is NP-hard (indeed APX-hard). Barman, Krishnamurthy, and Vaish (EC 2018) bypassed this via a market-based algorithm with price-rounding that computes an EF1+PO allocation in pseudo-polynomial time — polynomial in the magnitudes of the values rather than their bit-length — and proved (non-constructively, by a convergence argument) that EF1+fPO allocations always exist. Since then, the question of whether an EF1+PO (or EF1+fPO) allocation can be computed in truly polynomial time, with values given in binary, has become one of the best-known open computational problems in fair division, appearing in the Aziz–Li–Moulin–Wu survey and in the introductions of many follow-up papers. The problem is intriguing complexity-theoretically: existence is guaranteed, the problem lies in PLS (Garg–Murhekar), and no NP-hardness or PLS-hardness is known, so it sits in the same murky total-search territory as problems like computing a competitive equilibrium from equal incomes. A polynomial-time algorithm would make fair-and-efficient allocation practical in applications such as course allocation and public resource division; a hardness result would delineate a genuine computational price of combining fairness with efficiency.

What Is Known

Existence: max-Nash-welfare allocations are EF1+PO (Caragiannis, Kurokawa, Moulin, Procaccia, Shah, Wang, EC 2016 / TEAC 2019); EF1+fPO allocations always exist (Barman, Krishnamurthy, Vaish, EC 2018, via a non-constructive perturbation/convergence argument). Upper bounds: Barman–Krishnamurthy–Vaish (2018) compute EF1+PO in pseudo-polynomial time poly(n,m,Vmax⁡)\mathrm{poly}(n,m,V_{\max})poly(n,m,Vmax​); Garg and Murhekar (IJCAI 2021 / JAIR 2024, arXiv 2204.14229) strengthened this to a pseudo-polynomial algorithm for EF1+fPO, gave a strongly polynomial-time algorithm for kkk-ary instances (each agent has at most k=O(1)k=O(1)k=O(1) distinct values for the goods), a polynomial-time algorithm for EF1+PO when the number of agents nnn is constant, and showed the EF1+fPO problem is in PLS. The most notable recent progress is Mahara (arXiv 2411.01810, Nov 2024; SAGT 2025), who gave the first truly polynomial-time algorithm computing an EF1+fPO allocation for any FIXED number of agents (polynomial in mmm and log⁡Vmax⁡\log V_{\max}logVmax​ for constant nnn), by sequentially inserting agents while maintaining EF1+fPO. Special valuation classes are also solved: binary valuations (EFX+PO in polynomial time; Halpern et al. 2020, Babaioff–Ezra–Feige 2021), bi-valued valuations (EF1/EFX+PO in polynomial time; Garg–Murhekar 2023, Bu et al. 2024). Lower bounds: none — the problem is not known to be NP-hard or PLS-hard, and no super-polynomial lower bound is known in any model. Progress & Literature

Latest progress (Partial progress). The general question is still open as of July 2026, but there has been real movement on special cases since 2024: Mahara (arXiv 2411.01810, Nov 2024; published at SAGT 2025) gave the first truly polynomial-time algorithm for EF1+fPO with a fixed (constant) number of agents, strengthening Garg–Murhekar's constant-nnn EF1+PO result to the stronger fPO guarantee, via a new agent-by-agent insertion technique. Garg and Murhekar's JAIR 2024 journal version (arXiv 2204.14229) consolidated the pseudo-polynomial EF1+fPO algorithm, strongly polynomial algorithms for constantly many distinct values (kkk-ary instances), and PLS membership. For general nnn with values in binary, neither a polynomial-time algorithm nor any hardness result is known. A Polynomial-Time Algorithm for Fair and Efficient Allocation with a Fixed Number of Agents — Mahara, arXiv 2411.01810 (SAGT 2025) Computing Pareto-Optimal and Almost Envy-Free Allocations of Indivisible Goods — Garg, Murhekar, JAIR 2024 Finding Fair and Efficient Allocations — Barman, Krishnamurthy, Vaish, EC 2018

All known positive results flow from two techniques. The first is the Fisher-market / price-envy-freeness framework of Barman, Krishnamurthy, and Vaish (EC 2018): maintain an fPO allocation as a market equilibrium with item prices, and perform local swaps and price rises until price-EF1 (hence EF1) holds; a potential-function argument bounds the number of steps by poly(n,m,Vmax⁡)\mathrm{poly}(n,m,V_{\max})poly(n,m,Vmax​), which is only pseudo-polynomial because the potential moves in value-magnitude increments. Garg and Murhekar (IJCAI 2021 / JAIR 2024) refined this machinery to reach the stronger EF1+fPO guarantee in pseudo-polynomial time, to run in strongly polynomial time when agents have constantly many distinct values (the price space collapses), and to place the problem in PLS — currently the only nontrivial complexity classification. The second technique, due to Mahara (2024, SAGT 2025), is incremental: insert agents one at a time, restoring EF1+fPO after each insertion; the analysis is polynomial for each fixed number of agents, giving the first poly(m,log⁡Vmax⁡)\mathrm{poly}(m,\log V_{\max})poly(m,logVmax​) algorithm for constant nnn and the strongest partial result to date. Restricted valuation classes (binary, bi-valued) admit polynomial-time algorithms even for the stronger EFX+PO combination (Halpern et al. 2020; Babaioff–Ezra–Feige 2021; Garg–Murhekar 2023; Bu et al. 2024). The remaining gap is stark: for general nnn and binary-encoded additive values there is neither a polynomial-time algorithm nor any hardness evidence. New ideas seem needed either to make the market potential decrease geometrically (bit-scaling the prices is the obvious but so-far-unsuccessful route) or to prove PLS-hardness, which would be the first hardness result of its kind in fair division.
