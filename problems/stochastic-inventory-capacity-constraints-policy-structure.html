Short Statement

In a single-item periodic-review inventory system with fixed ordering costs and an order-capacity constraint, the natural continuous-order-property conjecture is false. What is the correct exact structural description of the optimal policy?

Open Problem

Give an exact structural characterization of optimal policies for capacitated stochastic inventory with fixed ordering costs, or prove that no finite threshold-like policy class can represent all optimal policies.

Background & Context

Fixed ordering costs and order-capacity constraints are both standard ingredients in inventory theory. Separately, they lead to fairly interpretable policies: fixed costs suggest (s,S)(s,S)(s,S) rules, while capacity constraints create gradual replenishment behavior. Together they produce a surprisingly delicate dynamic program. The state is only one-dimensional, so this is not a vague modelling problem or a curse-of-dimensionality artifact. The hard part is structural: the Bellman operator can create disconnected ordering regions and invalidate the most natural conjectures about when an order should be placed. This makes the problem a useful benchmark for operations-management theory, because a complete answer would say whether the classical threshold-policy paradigm survives once capacity and fixed charges are both present. Rossi, Chen, and Tarim give a numerical counterexample to the continuous order property, showing that the most obvious clean structure is false. The remaining question is not whether practitioners can compute good heuristics; it is whether the exact optimal policy has any parsimonious theorem-level description.

What Is Known

Classical uncapacitated fixed-cost inventory models admit (s,S)(s,S)(s,S)-type optimal policies under standard assumptions. Capacitated variants have only partial structural characterizations. Rossi, Chen, and Tarim (2023) show that if the continuous order property held, the optimal policy would have a modified multi-(s,S)(s,S)(s,S) form, but they also provide a numerical counterexample in which the continuous order property is violated. Their computations indicate that violations may be rare, and modified multi-(s,S)(s,S)(s,S) policies perform extremely well in tested instances. The exact general optimal-policy structure remains open.

The literature around this model is a good example of a mature operations-management problem where the modelling is settled but the structural theorem is missing. Classical fixed-cost inventory control suggests reorder intervals and order-up-to levels. Capacity constraints complicate this by preventing large corrective orders, so the Bellman recursion may favor ordering over multiple separated state regions. Rossi, Chen, and Tarim isolate the continuous order property as the condition under which a modified multi-(s,S)(s,S)(s,S) policy follows, then disprove that property by explicit numerical example. This leaves two possible directions: find a richer exact class that contains all optimal policies, or prove a negative result showing that no simple interval/threshold representation is possible without extra assumptions.
