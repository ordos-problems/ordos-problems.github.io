Short Statement

For Bayesian combinatorial auctions with independent subadditive buyers, a constant-factor prophet inequality is now known nonconstructively. Is there a constructive constant-factor posted-price prophet inequality, and what is the tight factor?

Open Problem

Design a polynomial-time computable posted-price/order-online policy with a constant-factor prophet inequality for subadditive combinatorial auctions, or prove that no such constructive pricing guarantee exists.

Background & Context

Subadditive combinatorial auctions are a central model for allocation without complementarities. There are mmm items and buyers arrive online with independently drawn subadditive valuations over bundles. The offline benchmark is the welfare-maximizing allocation after seeing all valuations. A prophet inequality asks whether a sequential allocation rule, ideally implementable by posted item prices, can obtain a constant fraction of this offline welfare in expectation. This is exactly the online-allocation analogue of simple truthful mechanisms for rich valuation classes. For XOS valuations, constant posted-price prophet inequalities are known, but subadditive valuations are substantially harder because there may be no compact additive support representation. Dütting, Kesselheim, and Lucier improved the constructive guarantee from O(log⁡m)O(\log m)O(logm) to O(log⁡log⁡m)O(\log\log m)O(loglogm) and explicitly conjectured that O(1)O(1)O(1) should be possible with unequal-marginal dual prices. Later work resolved the existence of a constant-factor subadditive prophet inequality nonconstructively, but the survey by Saha and Parashar emphasizes that this approach cannot be implemented using prices. The remaining question is therefore clean: close the gap between existence and a constructive posted-price mechanism.

What Is Known

Feldman, Gravin, and Lucier gave an O(log⁡m)O(\log m)O(logm) posted-price prophet inequality for subadditive valuations. Dütting, Kesselheim, and Lucier improved this to a constructive O(log⁡log⁡m)O(\log\log m)O(loglogm) guarantee using static anonymous item prices computable with demand-query access, and they prove their equal-marginal approach cannot beat Ω(log⁡log⁡m)\Omega(\log\log m)Ω(loglogm). They conjecture that unequal-marginal dual prices can yield an O(1)O(1)O(1) prophet inequality. Saha and Parashar's 2023 survey reports that later work resolves the central open problem of a constant-factor prophet inequality for subadditive valuations, but the known approach is nonconstructive and cannot be implemented using posted prices. Thus the constructive/pricing version and the tight constant remain open.

Remaining open problem. Design a polynomial-time computable posted-price/order-online policy with a constant-factor prophet inequality for subadditive combinatorial auctions, or prove that no such constructive pricing guarantee exists.

Progress & Literature

Latest progress (Partial progress). The constructive posted-price guarantee is O(log⁡log⁡m)O(\log\log m)O(loglogm). A constant-factor prophet inequality exists by later nonconstructive work, but this does not give computable posted prices or a simple mechanism. Dütting, Kesselheim, Lucier, O(log log m) prophet inequality Saha and Parashar survey on subadditive combinatorial auction prophet inequalities

The subadditive case exposes a gap between existential prophet inequalities and implementable posted-price mechanisms. Earlier balanced-price approaches give O(log⁡m)O(\log m)O(logm) because subadditive valuations are too coarse to support the XOS-style item decomposition used for constants. Dütting-Kesselheim-Lucier's primal-dual approach improves this to O(log⁡log⁡m)O(\log\log m)O(loglogm) by considering equal item marginals over a logarithmic ladder of scales; their own lower bound shows that this equal-marginal framework is exhausted. The natural next proof idea is unequal marginals derived from dual solutions of allocation LPs. The later nonconstructive constant-factor existence result shows that the model itself does not require logarithmic loss, but it leaves open the algorithmic and mechanism-design question that matters for posted prices.
