Short Statement

For periodic-review lost-sales inventory with positive deterministic lead time, characterize the exact optimal policy. Uniform approximation results are available, including a 2026 capped-base-stock bound, but do not give an exact policy.

Open Problem

Find sharper uniform policy guarantees or exact policy structure in the stated periodic-review lost-sales model. A concrete residual subquestion is whether the best base-stock policy is asymptotically optimal at fixed lead time as the penalty grows under continuous regularly varying demand tails; the known sublinear-mean-excess argument does not cover that class.

Background & Context

Periodic-review inventory theory with backlogged demand is one of the great success stories of operations research: for the backlog model the LLL-dimensional pipeline of outstanding orders collapses to the scalar inventory position, the dynamic program is convex, and a simple base-stock (order-up-to) policy is exactly optimal. Karlin and Scarf, in their foundational 1958 work, already observed that the seemingly innocuous change from backlogging to lost sales — unmet demand simply vanishes, as in brick-and-mortar and online retail, perishables, and spare parts, where customers walk or switch to a competitor — destroys this entire structure: even for lead time L=1L=1L=1 the optimal order is not a function of the inventory position alone, so base-stock optimality fails in general. Morton (1969) derived bounds relating the optimal policy to the backlog solution, but the exact dynamic program lives on the full LLL-dimensional pipeline vector and can be expensive to solve at larger lead times. Zipkin's influential 2008 papers revived the problem: using L♮L^{\natural}L♮-convexity he established monotonicity and bounded-sensitivity properties of the optimal order quantity, showed that neither base-stock nor constant-order is generally optimal, and showed numerically that base-stock policies can perform badly when the lead time is long and the penalty moderate. The problem then became the launching pad for the modern "asymptotic analysis of inventory systems" program surveyed by Goldberg, Reiman, and Wang (2021): if the exact optimum cannot be computed or described, prove that simple, interpretable policies are near-optimal in limiting regimes (large penalty, large lead time), and quantify the rate. Beyond its intrinsic role as perhaps the oldest unsolved structural question in inventory theory, the model is now a canonical benchmark for approximation guarantees in MDPs with high-dimensional states, for online learning with censored demand, and for deep-reinforcement-learning methods in operations, so any genuine structural characterization would reverberate well beyond inventory theory.

What Is Known

Dynamic programming retains the pipeline state and becomes computationally demanding as lead time grows; this is not a general complexity impossibility theorem. Partial structure is known: Karlin and Scarf (1958) showed base-stock is already suboptimal at L=1L=1L=1, and Zipkin (Operations Research 2008) proved via L♮L^{\natural}L♮-convexity that the optimal policy is monotone with bounded sensitivity in the pipeline vector but neither base-stock nor constant-order is generally optimal. Asymptotic regimes are well understood: (i) order-up-to/base-stock policies are asymptotically optimal as the lost-sales penalty p→∞p\to\inftyp→∞ under their demand-tail condition (Huh, Janakiraman, Muckstadt, Rusmevichientong, Management Science 2009); (ii) the constant-order policy is asymptotically optimal as L→∞L\to\inftyL→∞ (Goldberg, Katz-Rogozhnikov, Lu, Sharma, Squillante, Mathematics of OR 2016), with optimality gap decaying exponentially in LLL (Xin and Goldberg, Operations Research 2016). Capped base-stock policies (a base-stock rule with an order cap) interpolate between the two extremes: Xin (Operations Research 2021) showed they converge exponentially fast in the base-stock level to constant-order policies and perform robustly, and Xin (Operations Research 2022) proved an asymptotic worst-case guarantee for a continuous-review lost-sales model — a capped base-stock policy has asymptotic ratio at most 1.79 for the continuous-review model with Poisson demand (via an asymptotic analysis jointly in large penalty and lead time). Projected-inventory-level (PIL) policies are asymptotically optimal in BOTH classical regimes: as p→∞p\to\inftyp→∞ under Assumption 1 (bounded lead-time demand, or unbounded demand with sublinear conditional mean excess), and as L→∞L\to\inftyL→∞ for exponential demand (van Jaarsveld and Arts, Operations Research 2024); Moradi, Arts, and Drent (arXiv:2504.10132, April 2025) extended large-LLL asymptotic optimality of PIL to any demand with finite second moment provided the penalty is sufficiently large, via a Wiener-Hopf/ladder-process solution of the constant-order relative value function. Progress & Literature

Latest progress (Partial progress). The 2026 periodic-review CBS guarantee removes the alleged absence of uniform nonasymptotic bounds for that class. Exact policy characterization and sharper worst-case factors remain separate targets; continuous-review improvements require different assumptions. The fixed-lead-time, heavy-tail, large-penalty base-stock question is explicitly identified by Bu, Gong, and Chao (2024). Three kinds of progress must be kept separate: structural properties of exact optima, asymptotic optimality in limiting regimes, and uniform approximation factors. The existing base-stock, constant-order and projected-inventory analyses remain relevant, but the statement that nothing is known at finite parameters is false. The new CBS theorem directly addresses the periodic-review model. Continuous-review Poisson guarantees are related results, not substitutes for it; nor does a guarantee for the best policy within a restricted class characterize the unrestricted optimum. A second distinction is between bounded or sublinear-mean-excess demand and regularly varying tails. The large-penalty base-stock and PIL theorems cited here are not unconditional over all finite-mean demand. The heavy-tail question concerns the best base-stock policy at fixed lead time and is not settled by large-lead-time optimality of a different policy.
