Short Statement

In capacitated vehicle routing, the classic iterated tour partitioning gives ratio α+1\alpha+1α+1 (α\alphaα = best TSP ratio). A 2021 breakthrough shaved off a tiny amount; whether the +1+1+1 can be reduced - ideally to match TSP - is open.

Open Problem

Determine the optimal approximation ratio for unit-demand capacitated vehicle routing in general metrics, and in particular whether the classical α+1\alpha+1α+1 barrier can be substantially improved.

Background & Context

Capacitated vehicle routing is a fundamental routing model in operations research. In the unit-demand version, a depot and customers lie in a metric space, every customer has demand one, and each vehicle tour may visit at most QQQ customers before returning to the depot. The classical iterated tour partitioning algorithm starts with a TSP tour and cuts it into capacity-feasible pieces, yielding ratio roughly α+1\alpha+1α+1, where α\alphaα is the best metric-TSP approximation ratio. That guarantee stood for decades. Blauth, Traub, and Vygen gave the first improvement, shaving a fixed constant from the α+1\alpha+1α+1 bound for general, unit-demand, and splittable variants. The resulting open problem is how much of the extra +1+1+1 capacity loss is inherent, and whether CVRP approximability can be pushed much closer to TSP.

Statement & Model

In unit-demand capacitated vehicle routing, the input is a finite metric space with a distinguished depot rrr, a set of customers CCC, and an integer vehicle capacity QQQ. Each customer has demand one. A feasible solution is a collection of tours, each starting and ending at rrr, such that every customer is visited by some tour and each tour visits at most QQQ customers. The objective is to minimize total tour length. Let α\alphaα denote the best approximation ratio known or achievable for metric TSP. Classical iterated tour partitioning gives ratio α+1\alpha+1α+1 by building a TSP tour through all customers and cutting it into capacity-feasible routes. Determine the optimal approximation ratio for unit-demand CVRP in arbitrary metrics. In particular, decide whether one can substantially beat the tour-partitioning barrier, perhaps approaching the TSP ratio plus a smaller capacity-overhead term, or prove that a large additive loss over TSP is inherent.

What Is Known

Haimovich and Rinnooy Kan's iterated tour partitioning gives an α+1\alpha+1α+1 approximation for unit-demand CVRP, where α\alphaα is the approximation ratio for metric TSP; Altinkemer and Gavish obtained related partitioning guarantees. This was the benchmark for about 35 years. Blauth, Traub, and Vygen prove the first improvement, obtaining α+1−ϵ\alpha+1-\epsilonα+1−ϵ for an explicit positive constant, and their result applies to unit-demand CVRP, splittable CVRP, and general CVRP variants in arbitrary metrics. Hardness is much weaker and largely inherited from metric TSP, so the true threshold could be near α\alphaα, near α+1\alpha+1α+1, or somewhere between. Recent work improves other unsplittable CVRP variants, but the clean unit-demand approximability threshold remains open.

Remaining open problem. Determine the optimal approximation ratio for unit-demand capacitated vehicle routing in general metrics, and in particular whether the classical α+1\alpha+1α+1 barrier can be substantially improved.

Progress & Literature

Latest progress (Partial progress). The classical α+1\alpha+1α+1 barrier has been beaten by a small constant, but no near-tight algorithmic or hardness threshold is known. Blauth, Traub, Vygen, improving CVRP approximation Zhao and Xiao, improved unsplittable CVRP variants

The α+1\alpha+1α+1 guarantee separates routing cost into a TSP backbone plus depot-return/capacity overhead. The Blauth-Traub-Vygen improvement shows that this decomposition is not tight in the worst case, but the improvement is small. A major challenge is to integrate capacity decisions into the tour construction rather than partition after the fact. Lower bounds are also underdeveloped: known hardness does not explain an additive +1+1+1 loss. Progress may come from stronger route-covering relaxations, better randomized partitioning, or hard metric families where depot-distance and intercustomer-distance terms conflict.
