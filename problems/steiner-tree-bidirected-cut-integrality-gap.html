Short Statement

For the Steiner tree problem, the integrality gap of the bidirected-cut relaxation is conjectured to be close to 111; the best known lower bound is 6/5=1.26/5 = 1.26/5=1.2 and the best upper bound is 1.99881.99881.9988 (only shown below 222 in 2024).

Open Problem

Determine the exact integrality gap of the bidirected cut relaxation for graph Steiner tree.

Background & Context

The bidirected cut relaxation is one of the most natural LP relaxations for graph Steiner tree. It orients every undirected edge both ways, selects a root terminal, and requires one unit of directed capacity across every cut separating another terminal from the root. The relaxation is compact and performs well computationally, but for decades its theoretical integrality gap was stuck at the trivial upper bound 222, the same as the undirected cut relaxation. This changed only recently: Byrka, Grandoni, and Traub proved the first upper bound below 222, and Paschmanns and Traub later improved it substantially. The lower bound, however, is only 6/56/56/5. The question is now a clean LP-strength problem: how powerful is this canonical directed relaxation really?

What Is Known

When all vertices are terminals, the relaxation reduces to Edmonds-style arborescence/spanning-tree structure and is integral. For proper Steiner instances it can be fractional. The classical general upper bound was 222, matching standard undirected cut-relaxation reasoning and leaving open whether the directed formulation was theoretically stronger. Vicari constructed simplex-based instances showing gap at least 6/56/56/5, improving the earlier 36/3136/3136/31 lower bound. Byrka, Grandoni, and Traub proved the first nontrivial general upper bound below 222, namely 1.99881.99881.9988. Paschmanns and Traub later improved the upper bound to 1.8981.8981.898. For the special case in which a terminal minimum spanning tree is an optimal Steiner tree, they prove an upper bound 12/712/712/7 and show that a broad class of moat-growing algorithms cannot certify a bound below 12/712/712/7. Thus the exact general gap remains open in the interval [1.2,1.898][1.2,1.898][1.2,1.898], and current evidence suggests that closing it will require either stronger lower-bound gadgets or rounding methods beyond the known moat-growth framework.

Progress & Literature

Latest progress (Partial progress). The gap is now known to be below 222 and at most 1.8981.8981.898, while the best lower bound is 6/56/56/5. Moat-growing methods alone appear limited. Byrka, Grandoni, Traub, BCR gap below 2 Paschmanns, Traub, better BCR bounds and moat-growing limits Vicari, simplex lower bounds

The old 222 upper bound came from generic Steiner tree approximations and did not use enough structure of the directed relaxation. The new upper bounds use dual growth, terminal-moat structure, and refined accounting of how fractional directed cuts can be converted into an integral connecting subgraph. These methods prove that BCR is genuinely stronger than the undirected cut relaxation, but the improvement is still numerically modest compared with the best lower bound. Paschmanns and Traub's moat-growing barrier is especially informative: within a broad algorithmic template, one cannot certify a gap below 12/712/712/7 even in an important special case. The remaining gap can therefore be attacked from two directions. Lower-bound work can search for terminal-rich or simplex-like gadgets with BCR value much smaller than the integral tree. Upper-bound work likely needs rounding ideas that use global structure not visible to moat growth, or comparisons to stronger relaxations such as the hypergraphic relaxation.
