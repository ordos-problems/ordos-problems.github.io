Background & Context

Online bipartite matching began with Karp, Vazirani, and Vazirani (STOC 1990), who considered a bipartite graph whose offline side is known upfront while the other side arrives online, and showed that the randomized RANKING algorithm achieves the optimal competitive ratio 1−1/e≈0.6321-1/e \approx 0.6321−1/e≈0.632. Real markets, however, rarely have a static side: in ride-hailing, ride-sharing, and dynamic exchange platforms, agents on both sides of the market appear over time. Wang and Wong (ICALP 2015) therefore introduced the general (two-sided) vertex-arrival model, in which every vertex arrives online, revealing on arrival only its edges to previously arrived vertices, and the algorithm must decide immediately whether to match the newcomer to an unmatched earlier vertex or leave it for the future. The defining difficulty, absent from both the one-sided model and the related fully online model of Huang et al. (JACM 2020), is that the algorithm is never told when a vertex has seen its last edge, so it can never safely 'greedily finish' a vertex. Wang and Wong showed a fractional algorithm beating the trivial greedy ratio of 1/21/21/2, and Gamlath, Kapralov, Maggiori, Svensson, and Wajc (FOCS 2019) resolved the qualitative question for integral matching, proving that randomization beats 1/21/21/2 by an absolute constant — in sharp contrast to the even more general edge-arrival model, where they showed 1/21/21/2 is optimal. The model has since become one of the canonical testbeds in online matching: it sits strictly between one-sided arrivals (ratio 1−1/e1-1/e1−1/e) and edge arrivals (ratio 1/21/21/2), it separates cleanly from the fully online model, and its analysis has driven the development of primal-dual techniques for two-sided markets and of online rounding methods for converting fractional matchings into integral ones. In February 2026 Zhihao Gavin Tang pinned down the exact optimal ratio for the fractional version, leaving the integral question as the central remaining open problem.

Statement & Model

Consider a graph G=(V,E)G=(V,E)G=(V,E), initially unknown, whose vertices arrive online in adversarial order. Upon the arrival of a vertex vvv, its edges to previously arrived vertices are revealed; the algorithm must either match vvv immediately to a currently unmatched, previously arrived neighbor, or leave vvv unmatched (in which case vvv may still be matched later, upon the arrival of a future neighbor uuu, at uuu's arrival time). All matching decisions are irrevocable. The objective is the cardinality of the produced matching, compared against the maximum matching of the final graph; an algorithm is Γ\GammaΓ-competitive if it always attains at least Γ\GammaΓ times the offline optimum (in expectation, for randomized algorithms). When GGG is bipartite this is the two-sided online bipartite matching model of Wang and Wong (ICALP 2015); on general graphs it is called online matching with general vertex arrivals. What Is Known

Zhihao Gavin Tang, 'Optimal Competitive Ratio of Two-sided Online Bipartite Matching' (arXiv:2602.18049v1, 2026), Theorem 1.1, proves a fractional impossibility bound of Γ∗≈0.526\Gamma^*\approx0.526Γ∗≈0.526. This matches the fractional algorithms of Wang and Wong (2015) and Tang and Zhang (2024), resolving that variant. For randomized integral matching, Gamlath, Kapralov, Maggiori, Svensson, and Wajc (2019) achieve 1/2+ε1/2+\varepsilon1/2+ε for an absolute constant ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0 on general graphs. The reviewed results leave the integral optimum between this guarantee and Γ∗\Gamma^*Γ∗. Tang's upper bound applies because the expected edge indicators of a randomized integral algorithm form a feasible fractional matching. The fractional theorem does not supply a lossless online rounding algorithm. Nor should guarantees from fully online matching be transferred here: that model reveals departure deadlines and permits decisions until departure. In the present model, an edge must be selected when its later endpoint arrives.

Work on general vertex arrivals has proceeded on three fronts. (1) Fractional algorithms: Wang and Wong (ICALP 2015) introduced the model and beat greedy with a dual-based 0.5260.5260.526-competitive fractional algorithm; Tang and Zhang (EC 2024) matched this with a cleaner primal water-filling-style algorithm characterized by a non-increasing threshold function a(⋅)a(\cdot)a(⋅) and a primal-dual certificate g(⋅)g(\cdot)g(⋅). (2) Hardness: successive adversary constructions lowered the ceiling from 0.6250.6250.625 (Wang-Wong) to 0.59140.59140.5914 (Buchbinder, Segev, Tkach) to 0.5840.5840.584 (Tang-Zhang EC 2024), and finally Tang (arXiv:2602.18049, 2026) built a recursive construction — repeatedly confronting the algorithm with the dilemma of matching now versus waiting for perfect partners that may never come — proving the Tang-Zhang certificate optimal and pinning the fractional optimum at exactly Γ∗≈0.526\Gamma^*\approx0.526Γ∗≈0.526. (3) Integral algorithms: Gamlath, Kapralov, Maggiori, Svensson, and Wajc (FOCS 2019) rounded the Wang-Wong algorithm online to get 12+Ω(1)\tfrac12+\Omega(1)21​+Ω(1), the only non-trivial integral result to date; their rounding is lossy and the resulting constant is minuscule. The remaining gap is therefore [12+Ω(1),0.526][\tfrac12+\Omega(1), 0.526][21​+Ω(1),0.526] for randomized integral algorithms. New ideas seem needed on both sides: either lossless-style online rounding adapted to two-sided arrivals (the rounding barriers studied by Buchbinder, Naor, and Wajc, SODA 2023, are directly relevant), or integral-specific hardness beating 0.5260.5260.526, which cannot come from fractional constructions.
