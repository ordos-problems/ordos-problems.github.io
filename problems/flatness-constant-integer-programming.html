Short Statement

Determine the correct asymptotic order of the maximum lattice width of lattice-free convex bodies.

Open Problem

Close the gap between the best lower and upper bounds on Flt⁡(d)\operatorname{Flt}(d)Flt(d); in particular, decide whether the flatness constant is linear in ddd.

Background & Context

Flatness is a geometric form of a basic integer-programming principle: if a convex relaxation contains no integer point, then it must be thin in some integer direction. Lenstra's algorithm and its descendants exploit this by branching on a bounded number of integer hyperplanes. Better flatness bounds translate into sharper fixed-dimension integer-programming algorithms and into structural information about maximal lattice-free convex sets, which are also central in cutting-plane theory. The model is parsimonious and fully formal: no stochastic assumptions, no application-specific modeling choices, just convex bodies, lattice points, and width. This makes it exactly the kind of hard, clean OR/open mathematics problem the repository is meant to track.

What Is Known

Classical geometry-of-numbers arguments prove finiteness of Flt⁡(d)\operatorname{Flt}(d)Flt(d) and polynomial bounds in ddd. Kannan's fixed-dimension integer-programming work and the Kannan-Lovasz covering-minima framework connect flatness to algorithmic integer programming. Dadush's subspace flatness conjecture predicted an essentially logarithmic factor in a related covering-radius comparison, which would imply near-optimal flatness and faster integer programming. Reis and Rothvoss proved this conjecture up to a constant in the exponent, obtaining an upper bound that implies Flt⁡(d)=O(dlog⁡3(2d))\operatorname{Flt}(d)=O(d\log^3(2d))Flt(d)=O(dlog3(2d)). The flatness constant is a direct bridge between geometry of numbers and exact integer optimization. Kannan and Lovasz introduced covering-minima tools that made flatness algorithmically meaningful. Dadush's subspace flatness conjecture pointed to an almost optimal route through covering-radius lower bounds, and Reis-Rothvoss nearly realized it with near-linear polylogarithmic consequences.
