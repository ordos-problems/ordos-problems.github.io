Background & Context

Bimatrix games are one of the cleanest mathematical models of strategic interaction in economics. They are also a central source problem for PPAD, the complexity class that captures total search problems proved total by parity or fixed-point arguments. Exact Nash equilibrium computation is hard, so approximation became the practical and theoretical fallback. The approximation question is subtle because coarse constant approximations are easy, quasi-polynomial algorithms can achieve arbitrarily small constant error, and PPAD lower bounds become much stronger when the allowed running time is quasi-polynomial rather than polynomial. The historical 1/31/31/3 barrier is important because it is a concrete, model-independent number in the simplest two-player setting. Moving it in either direction would sharpen the computational meaning of Nash equilibrium: either general games admit better polynomial-time strategic predictions than currently known, or the hardness of equilibrium appears already at a visible constant. A new preprint claims to cross it; the overall quantitative threshold is still unresolved.

What Is Known

Daskalakis, Goldberg, and Papadimitriou and independently Chen, Deng, and Teng established PPAD-completeness for exact Nash equilibrium computation, including the two-player case. Lipton, Markakis, and Mehta showed that ε\varepsilonε-approximate equilibria can be found in quasi-polynomial time by enumerating small supports. Tsaknakis and Spirakis gave a long-standing polynomial-time algorithm with guarantee about 0.3393+δ0.3393+\delta0.3393+δ. Deligkas, Fasoulakis, and Markakis improved this to 1/3+δ1/3+\delta1/3+δ in polynomial time by refining the descent framework and using a richer set of candidate strategies. Progress & Literature

Approximate Nash computation has two different frontiers. The quasi-polynomial frontier is nearly understood: small-support enumeration gives an nO(log⁡n)n^{O(\log n)}nO(logn) algorithm for any fixed error, and Rubinstein gives matching quasi-polynomial lower bounds for some constant error under PPAD-ETH. The polynomial-time frontier is much sharper numerically and much less understood. The Tsaknakis-Spirakis descent framework dominated for years at 0.3393+δ0.3393+\delta0.3393+δ, and Deligkas-Fasoulakis-Markakis pushed it to 1/3+δ1/3+\delta1/3+δ. A further improvement likely needs either a new structural theorem about approximate best responses in arbitrary bimatrix games or a stronger bottleneck analysis of descent-style methods. A hardness result near 1/31/31/3 would require reductions that preserve a much larger approximation constant than current PPAD-hardness technology provides. The new preprint claims a further descent-framework improvement. Do not confuse this general-game result with stronger guarantees restricted to symmetric games.
