Polynomial-Time Complexity of the Square-Root-Sum Problem

Short Statement

Many Euclidean optimization algorithms need to compare sums of distances exactly; the basic Turing-model complexity of comparing integer square-root sums is still unresolved.

Open Problem

Decide whether the square-root-sum problem is solvable in polynomial time on a Turing machine.

Background & Context

This is a small-looking numerical problem with outsized consequences for computational geometry and geometric optimization. Lengths of Euclidean paths, tours, triangulations, matchings, and Steiner or network-design objects are often sums of square roots of integers. Approximation algorithms can usually avoid exact comparison, but exact decision problems and membership in classes such as NP can depend on whether one can compare such sums within polynomially many bits. For example, the exact complexity of minimum-weight triangulation is entangled with this issue: NP-hardness is known, but NP membership for the Euclidean-length decision version is delicate because a certificate may require comparing sums of radicals. The problem is therefore not about choosing a model of a real-world system; the model is a finite integer expression, and the hard part is the bit-complexity of exact sign determination.

Conversely, proving that no such algorithm exists under standard complexity assumptions would separate this exact numerical-comparison problem from the real-RAM intuition that square roots can be manipulated at unit cost.

What Is Known

The problem is easy in a unit-cost real-RAM model but its bit-complexity on Turing machines is open. Allender, Burgisser, Kjeldgaard-Pedersen, and Miltersen placed square-root-sum inside the counting hierarchy, giving a strong upper bound well below arbitrary PSPACE reasoning but not a polynomial-time algorithm. Separation-bound approaches give algorithms whose running time depends on lower bounds for the smallest nonzero value of a square-root sum. Burnikel, Fleischer, Mehlhorn, and Schirra proved a strong computable separation bound for radical expressions, but the dependence remains exponential in the number of terms. Cheng, Meng, Sun, and Chen related the relevant separation quantity to lattice reduction and gave conditional and experimental evidence for sharper behavior. Eisenbrand, Haeberle, and Singer used the subspace theorem to improve the bound to a singly exponential form for fixed radicands, but their constant is ineffective and the result still does not yield a general polynomial-time algorithm. Thus the open part is still the same sharp question: can signs of arbitrary integer square-root sums be decided in polynomial time?

Remaining open problem. Decide whether the square-root-sum problem is solvable in polynomial time on a Turing machine.

Progress & Literature

Latest progress (Partial progress). The problem lies in the counting hierarchy and admits exponential or special-case separation bounds, including a 2023 subspace-theorem improvement, but no polynomial-time algorithm or hardness barrier is known. Eisenbrand-Haeberle-Singer 2023 Cheng-Meng-Sun-Chen lattice-reduction paper

The square-root-sum problem is a bridge between exact numerical computation and discrete optimization. Its input is completely finite and integer-valued, yet deciding the sign may require ruling out extremely small nonzero gaps between algebraic sums. Allender and coauthors gave a structural upper bound through the counting hierarchy, showing that the problem is not arbitrary real computation. Separation-bound work supplies algorithms by proving that nonzero sums cannot be too close to zero, but known bounds still lead to superpolynomial precision in the number of terms. The Eisenbrand-Haeberle-Singer bound is a real advance for fixed radicands, but because its constant is ineffective and radicands vary with the input, it does not settle the original polynomial-time question. The remaining gap is therefore exact and clean: either prove a polynomial bit-complexity comparison algorithm, likely via stronger effective separation, or show that such a comparison would imply an unexpected complexity collapse.
