Is unknown uniformly random order ever worse than worst-case known order?

Short Statement

Intuitively, an algorithm facing values in unknown uniformly random order should do at least as well as facing an adversary's worst order that it is told in advance. The open question: can this ever fail for some product distribution? Resolving that it never does would derive random-order prophet inequalities from fixed-order analysis.

Open Problem

Decide whether unknown uniformly random order is always at least as good as the worst deterministic order revealed in advance, in the single-choice product-distribution prophet model.

Background & Context

Arrival order is one of the main axes along which prophet inequalities vary. In a fixed-order model, the algorithm knows the order and can tailor thresholds to it. In an adversarial-order model, a malicious order is chosen. In the random-order or prophet-secretary model, the order is uniformly random but unknown in advance. Intuition says that an unknown random order should not be worse than the worst deterministic order revealed to the algorithm: randomness should average over bad permutations rather than create a new disadvantage. Will Ma's problem asks whether this intuition is actually true for single-choice product distributions. The subtlety is that the known-order benchmark may choose a different optimal policy for each permutation, whereas the random-order algorithm must commit to one strategy before seeing the permutation. This is an interchange-of-information question rather than a new stochastic model. If the inequality always holds, random-order prophet analyses could sometimes be reduced to simpler fixed-order arguments.

What Is Known

Prophet-secretary work shows that random order can improve competitive ratios relative to adversarial order in standard prophet models, with threshold-based guarantees above 1/21/21/2 in several settings. Ezra, Feldman, Gravin, and Tang formalize the value of knowing the arrival order through the order-competitive ratio and prove tight bounds for several prophet settings and algorithm classes. Their results show that order-awareness can matter, but they do not directly settle Will Ma's sharper minimax comparison: can the expectation over unknown uniformly random order be strictly worse than the minimum over revealed deterministic orders? No counterexample or general theorem for Equation (C.1) is recorded in the current site sources.

Progress & Literature

Latest progress (Partial progress). Related order-awareness losses have been characterized in other formulations, but the exact minimax comparison in Equation (C.1) remains open. Ezra et al., significance of knowing arrival order

Prophet-secretary results show that random arrival order can be algorithmically helpful, but they optimize a standard competitive ratio and do not compare against a worst revealed order. Recent work on arrival-order knowledge shows that order information can matter, yet Will Ma's formulation asks a more surgical minimax question. The known-order side optimizes after seeing the permutation; the random-order side optimizes before the permutation is drawn. This creates a nontrivial interchange of min, expectation, and sup. A positive result might come from convexity or a symmetrization argument showing that a randomized mixture of known-order policies can be implemented order-unaware. A negative result would identify a product distribution where policy specialization across permutations is essential.
